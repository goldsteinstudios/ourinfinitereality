Ferrofluid sphere

A glass sphere coated in ferrofluid. Magnets sweep around it, pulling the fluid up as they pass. The question: can it be pulled up strongly enough, or often enough, that it never has time to fall back?

The film is simulated with real material properties: viscosity, surface tension, magnetization, gravity, and the field of an actual cylinder magnet. Drag the view to orbit it and scroll to zoom. The verdict is measured along the whole path the magnets sweep. The purple dot is one point on that path, and its history is plotted below.

What's being modelled

The film

Film thickness is tracked on about 2,600 patches covering the sphere. Fluid flows between patches, driven by:

  • The Kelvin force μ₀M∇H, which pulls magnetized fluid toward stronger field. Magnetization follows a Langevin curve that saturates at Ms.
  • Magnetic normal stress μ₀Mn²/2 on the free surface.
  • Surface tension, which flattens bumps, and gravity. By default gravity points to the sphere's centre, so it presses the coat evenly onto the glass and pulls every raised bump back down. With table gravity instead, the coat drains down the sides and drips off the bottom.
  • Viscous drag from the glass (3μv/h²) and the fluid's inertia. Thick, thin fluids slosh. Thin, viscous films creep.

The spikes

Above a critical normal magnetization Mc (the Rosensweig instability), a ferrofluid surface breaks into spikes spaced about 2π√(σ/ρg) apart. Spike height is limited by the fluid available, rises and collapses at the film's own relaxation rate, and shows hysteresis: once formed, spikes survive down to about 0.9 Mc. Spikes are drawn leaning along the local field.

The magnets

Each magnet is a solid cylinder, modelled by spreading magnetic charge over both pole faces. Its axis points at the sphere's centre, and the gap is measured from the glass to the near face.

When fluid leaves

A fluid column detaches when the outward force on it beats surface tension (Bond number ρ·f·h²/σ ≳ 3.5). With table gravity, fluid on the underside drips. Centre gravity never pulls fluid off. Under a strong, close magnet, the fluid jumps to the magnet. Both count as fluid lost.

Earth-like mode

The sphere spins on a tilted axis while the magnet, standing in for the Sun, goes round it once a year. The orbit can be eccentric: the gap changes and the Sun moves fastest at its closest point, following Kepler's law. The axis can also precess (trace a cone) and nod, the way Earth's tilt changes over 41,000 years. Time is compressed, and the Sun here pulls magnetically, which real sunlight does not. What it tests is the pattern: whether a mobile medium follows a tilted, periodic forcing between hemispheres, and with what delay.

Limits

This is a thin-film model. The bulk shape is computed, but individual spikes are a sub-grid estimate, and the fluid's own effect on the field (demagnetization) is included only in the normal direction. Centrifugal force from spinning the sphere is negligible at these speeds and is left out. Material presets are typical datasheet values, not specific products. Trends and orders of magnitude are trustworthy. Exact thresholds are approximate to within tens of percent.
